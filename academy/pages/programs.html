Foundations · Suggested ages 5–10

The joy of discovery

Develop confidence with language, numbers and observation. Short, repeatable activities help young learners build a vocabulary for the world around them.

What to explore

  • Read a story aloud and retell it in your own words.
  • Count, group and compare everyday objects.
  • Keep a nature notebook with drawings and dates.
  • Make art, music and simple models.

A project to try

A week of noticing. Choose a tree or patch of sky. Draw what you see at the same time each day. Write one question beneath each drawing, then compare the week.

Work with a trusted adult, stay in familiar safe spaces and avoid touching unknown plants or animals.

Connections · Suggested ages 11–13

The art of questioning

Move from collecting facts to explaining relationships. Practice reading different accounts, representing ideas with numbers and testing a claim fairly.

What to explore

  • Compare two accounts of one historical event.
  • Use ratios, graphs and simple equations.
  • Distinguish a claim, evidence and reasoning.
  • Discuss a story from another character’s view.

A project to try

One question, three sources. Pick a question about your local environment. Find three reliable sources, record the author and date, and compare where they agree or differ.

Present your conclusion in one page, including what you still do not know.

Perspectives · Suggested ages 14–18

A voice of your own

Bring careful reading and disciplined inquiry to a question that matters to you. Learn to revise a position, communicate a result and acknowledge uncertainty.

What to explore

  • Read sustained works of literature and nonfiction.
  • Model real problems with mathematics.
  • Analyze primary sources and scientific evidence.
  • Develop and revise a longer research project.

A project to try

A small public contribution. Investigate a local question using public information. Create a short guide, data story or essay for a specific audience.

Keep a source log, explain limitations and ask a peer to challenge your argument before revising.

Make the pathway your own.

Begin with a manageable question, choose an appropriate resource and agree on what a finished piece of work will look like. Keep sessions short enough to stay focused, and leave time to explain or revise what you learned.

These are suggested independent activities. They do not provide course credit, assessment, qualifications or personalized instruction. Families should choose materials appropriate to the learner.
